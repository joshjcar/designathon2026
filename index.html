Your calendar knows the deadline. It has no idea when you needed to start.

Startline reads your semester backwards — your real free hours, the work each assessment actually takes, and everything competing for the same evenings — then hands back the last honest day you can begin.

Open the planner Read the research

Three inputs, one answer

No account, no sync, no notifications begging for attention. You say what you owe and how much time you have; it does arithmetic you were never going to do by hand.

1

List what you owe

Every assessment across every course, with its deadline, what it's worth, and an honest guess at the hours. Guessing badly is fine — change one number and watch the whole semester rearrange.

2

Declare your real week

Not the 40 hours you imagine having. The 12 or 15 that survive lectures, work, commuting and sleep. Every date below is built on that one honest number.

3

Collect your startlines

Each task gets the last day you can begin it. Where two tasks want the same hours, the clash is already resolved — so the dates you see are ones you can actually keep.

Why start dates, and not better reminders

Students are not short of deadline reminders. The research points somewhere else entirely.

80–95%

of university students procrastinate on coursework, across a meta-analysis of the field. It is the normal condition of studying, not a flaw in a minority.

Steel, P. (2007). Psychological Bulletin, 133(1), 65–94.

55.5 vs 48.6

Students writing a thesis took 55.5 days on average. Their own worst-case estimate had been 48.6 days. The pessimistic guess was still optimistic.

Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994). Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381.

d = 0.65

Deciding in advance when you will act has a medium-to-large effect on whether you do it, across 94 independent tests. That is the single lever this tool pulls.

Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006). Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119.
